# DECISIONS

## D1: Postgres as the single source of truth
Chose: PostgreSQL for jobs, leases, fencing tokens, and worker registry.
Over: Redis + a separate durable log (e.g. Redis Streams or NATS JetStream).
Because: Three coordinators need a shared, transactional view of "this job is pending / dispatched / done" and a globally monotonic fencing sequence. Postgres gives us `SELECT … FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` for safe multi-replica dispatch, a real `SEQUENCE` for fencing tokens that survives coordinator restarts and leader changes, and `UNIQUE(IdempotencyKey)` for cross-coordinator deduplication — all in one round trip per job, no extra coordination protocol.
Cost: One DB is a single failure domain and a throughput ceiling. At >5–10k jobs/sec we'd hit row-lock contention on the head of the pending queue, and the harness `partition_db` fault literally fails every query on the affected coordinator (which is the desired behavior — but in production we'd need a read replica + PgBouncer + likely partitioning by job hash).

## D2: WebSocket from worker → coordinator
Chose: A single outbound WebSocket per worker, multiplexed for dispatch / result / ack / control messages.
Over: gRPC bidi streaming, or raw framed TCP with a hand-rolled protocol.
Because: The spec mandates outbound-only (NAT-friendly) and one persistent connection. WS gives us framing, ping/pong keepalive, and trivial JSON message envelopes for free in both Node runtimes (`ws`) — no `.proto` toolchain, no length-prefix parser to debug under chaos. Multiplexing dispatch + control + result over the same socket means concurrency-limit changes (`control.set_concurrency`) are delivered in-order with respect to dispatches, so we never race a new limit against a job that's already on the wire.
Cost: JSON-over-WS is ~3–5× slower to encode/parse than protobuf and gives no schema enforcement at the wire. We rely on TypeScript discriminated unions for safety, which means a misbehaving client could send garbage and we'd only catch it at runtime. A gRPC stream would also give us built-in backpressure; WS gives us none, so we explicitly cap in-flight per worker and let the coordinator re-dispatch on overflow.

## D3: Fencing token = Postgres `BIGSERIAL` (global sequence)
Chose: One `fencing_seq` sequence shared by every coordinator; `nextval()` is called inside the dispatch transaction.
Over: Per-coordinator counter + coordinator-id prefix, or a Hybrid Logical Clock.
Because: The spec is explicit — tokens must be strictly monotonic across the entire system, across restarts, across replicas, and unaffected by wall-clock skew. A Postgres sequence is the simplest construct that gives all four for free: it lives outside any coordinator process, `nextval` is atomic and increasing under concurrent access, and the `clock_skew` chaos fault literally cannot move it backward because it doesn't touch time. Workers send the token back with every commit; the coordinator compares against the lease row's token; stale workers are silently rejected.
Cost: Every dispatch is a synchronous DB round trip for `nextval`, even when nothing else changed. Under partition_db the coordinator can't dispatch at all (fail-closed, by design). A per-coordinator counter would be partition-tolerant but would require a separate epoch/leader-election protocol to stay globally monotonic across restarts — exactly the complexity we're avoiding.

## D4: Short lease TTL extended by worker heartbeats
Chose: 15-second lease TTL, extended by the worker sending a `job.heartbeat` (with jobId + fencing token) every 5 seconds over the existing WebSocket. The coordinator extends `Lease.ExpiresAt` only if `(jobId, workerId, token)` matches a live lease row. The reaper runs every 5s on every coordinator and resets expired-lease jobs back to PENDING. The worker never reads its own clock — lease validity is decided only at commit time by the coordinator comparing tokens.
Over: A long fixed TTL (e.g. 60s) with no heartbeats, or workers self-checking `Date.now() > leaseExpiresAt` and aborting.
Because: The spec forbids worker wall-clock expiry checks (up to 5min skew) and the harness drains for 45s after submission — so recovery on worker death has to fit in <45s. With heartbeats, a healthy worker keeps its lease alive indefinitely (so 30-second jobs never lose their lease), but a dead worker stops heartbeating and is reaped in ≤15s TTL + ≤5s reaper = ≤20s, well inside the drain window. A long fixed TTL trades the other way: long jobs are safe but a SIGKILL'd worker's leases sit unreaped for the full TTL. The "I finished but my lease was reaped" case still works correctly — the coordinator sees the token mismatch and discards the late result; the re-leased copy carries the work forward.
Cost: Heartbeats add ~1 DB UPDATE per in-flight job per 5s — at peak 80 in-flight that's 16 writes/sec across the cluster, tiny but non-zero. A worker that's CPU-pegged enough to miss two consecutive heartbeats will be reaped mid-execution and its work duplicated. Picking the right TTL/cadence ratio (3×) is empirical: tighter and we false-reap on GC pauses, looser and worker death takes longer to detect.

## D5: Leaderless coordinators (no Raft, no election)
Chose: All three coordinators are peers. Each runs its own dispatch loop and lease reaper, contention is resolved at the row level by `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`.
Over: Run an election (Raft via etcd, or a Postgres advisory-lock-based "primary") so only one coordinator dispatches at a time.
Because: With SKIP LOCKED, two coordinators racing to dispatch the same pending row will pick different rows — no double-dispatch, no coordination protocol, no leader-failover blackout window. The system survives SIGKILL of any coordinator immediately: the other two keep dispatching, the reaper recovers any in-flight leases held by the dead coordinator's workers within one reaper tick. `/stats` reports `mode: leaderless`. Operationally this is one less thing to debug at 3am.
Cost: We give up the natural place to put "exactly-one" logic (cron-like maintenance jobs, single-writer metrics aggregation). Every coordinator polls for pending work every 200ms — 3× the DB load of a single-leader design. We also drop the `leader_term` field from `/stats` (the spec's example shows it, but reporting a fake number on a leaderless design would mislead operators). The trade is justified for this workload but would not be for one with strict per-tenant ordering.
